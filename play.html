Play and Learn

Four games and an examination

In each game the rules are Bentham's argument: you weigh pleasures on his seven circumstances, move money until the good it does runs out, and rescue a town whose savings have gone into a chest. They unlock in order, because each one needs the one before it.

i

The Felicific Calculus

Sit as Bentham's legislator and weigh real choices: a feast now or a pension later, a law that pains one man and pleases many. One new circumstance each round.

You will discover: how intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent combine, and why the last of them was the one later economists dropped.

Play
ii

The Labourer and the Richest Man

Take Bentham's own guess about pleasure and wealth, then move money from the rich man to ten labourers. Find where the good stops, then see the same chart through Senior's eyes.

You will discover: diminishing marginal utility of money, why it argues for redistribution, why Bentham still stopped short of equality, and why later economists refused to compare utilities at all.

Play
iii

The Money in the Chest

First believe, with young Bentham and Say, that every saved pound becomes capital. Then watch frugality turn into a slump, and advise the government how much money to issue, year by year.

You will discover: why later Bentham rejected Say's law, what single assumption separates them, and how close he came to arguments made by Malthus and Keynes.

Play
iv

The Tribunal of Utility

You are Bentham. Smith, Say, Senior, Hunt, Marx, and J. S. Mill each bring a charge. Answer in Bentham's own terms, then state their case more strongly than they did.

You will discover: where Bentham stands on value, gluts, inequality, and government, and why he lands on both sides of the course question.

Play

The Examination

Twelve questions, one attempt each, with numbers drawn fresh every time. Ten right earns a printable certificate. Opens after all four games and the Key Ideas.
