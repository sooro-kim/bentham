Critics & Legacy

Who fought Bentham, and who carried him forward

Ten thinkers, from a forerunner in 1651 to a 1997 paper titled “Back to Bentham?” Each card stays half-closed until you name the Benthamite doctrine that person attacked or built on. You can only do that if you know the doctrines.

Harmony or conflict?

Bentham answers this course's question twice. The early Bentham, writing in the late eighteenth century, is a writer of harmony. Each person seeks his own pleasure, and the free market allocates resources “in the most socially beneficial manner possible,” in Hunt's phrase: “no regulations nor any efforts whatsoever … can raise the quantity of wealth produced.” Nor can there be a glut: “Whoever saves money,” he wrote, “adds proportionately to the general mass of capital,” the belief later called Say's law. Hunt says the utility tradition's “perceived social harmony of all persons' interests” rests on everyone's dependence on the market (pp. 127, 132, 137).

The later Bentham, by 1801, sees two places where the market leaves happiness on the table. Money saved may be “put into a chest and kept there,” so that prices, production, and profits fall. And because each extra pound brings less pleasure, a labourer and a man fifty thousand times richer cannot be fifty thousand times apart in happiness: “Five times the labourer's seems a very large, not to say excessive allowance.” So he called in “the hand of government,” though he stopped short of equality, since the labourer still needs an inducement to labour. His benchmark was the inequality of “the Anglo-American United States: meaning always those in which slave-holding has no place,” which Hunt notes was then substantially less than England's (pp. 132–34).

Notice what kind of conflict this is. In Hunt's account Bentham does not speak of classes whose incomes come from opposite sources, as Ricardo's wages and profits do. He speaks of rich and poor persons whose pleasures can be added up, with a fair legislator to settle the account. Hunt's objection is that, on Bentham's own theory, legislators are egoists too, and must ask whether they gain more by serving the poor or the rich (p. 134). The successors chose sides. Senior struck out the premises behind redistribution and kept the harmony (pp. 144–45, 149); Robbins later ruled the comparison out of science. Hunt adds that Bentham's own split, laissez-faire against reform, reappears inside neoclassical economics (p. 134). Hunt himself reads the whole utility tradition as a way of removing the class conflict that Smith's and Ricardo's labour theory had exposed (p. 127). So where does Bentham land? He founded the theory of harmony, and then, from inside it, gave the first utilitarian reasons to doubt it.
